# User needs and barriers

Evaluate barriers in real tasks: understanding information, navigating, completing forms, operating controls and perceiving state. A markup defect matters through its user impact.

## Blind users

Access methods include screen readers, refreshable Braille displays, keyboards, text-to-speech and structural navigation through headings, landmarks, lists, links, tables and controls. Do not assume a single device or navigation mode.

Relevant barriers include missing or misleading image alternatives; nonsemantic clickable elements; unnamed controls; missing form relationships; unannounced dynamic status; visual-only errors; inaccessible dialogs; keyboard traps; illogical focus order; inconsistent hidden-content exposure; missing table relationships; unexposed widget role/state/value; charts without equivalent data or explanation; and prerecorded video whose essential visuals are absent from audio description or the applicable alternative.

## Low-vision users

Access may involve browser zoom, text enlargement, magnification, custom colors, forced-colors/high-contrast modes, custom spacing, large pointers or a keyboard with magnification.

Relevant barriers include weak text/UI contrast; horizontal scrolling under zoom; clipping, overlap and fixed-height text containers; sticky layers obscuring focus; hover content that cannot be dismissed or reached; tiny dense targets; images of text; and invisible focus indicators. Preserve user settings rather than forcing one presentation.

## Color-vision-deficient users

Color perception varies; do not reduce it to a single red/green condition. Errors, required fields, selected states, statuses, charts and links can become ambiguous when differentiated only by hue or insufficient contrast. Text, patterns, shapes, markers and other meaningful visual cues can supplement color.

## Variability and intersection

Users may have multiple disabilities or access needs. Technologies, settings, preferences and abilities differ. Do not infer an individual's competence, preferred input or device from a disability label. Passing a technical rule does not prove a good experience. Include disabled-user evaluation of actual tasks alongside technical and AT checks.

See [W3C visual barriers](https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/abilities-barriers/visual/) for background and [the rule index](rule-index.md) for this phase's coverage.
